## Section 1 — What We Built

MummSTER is a database and artificial intelligence system for Philadelphia Mummers string band competition history. At its core, it is a single place where every competition result, every judge's score, every award, and every parade fact that was previously scattered across spreadsheets, PDFs, and website popups has been collected, structured, and made searchable.

The AI layer — the part you actually talk to — lets you ask questions in plain English and get answers backed by the data. You don't need to know SQL. You don't need to know what a database is. You type "Which string band has won the most first prizes since 1991?" and you get an answer with a table, a chart, and an explanation of the result.

Why does this matter for the Mummers community? Because string band history has always lived in people's heads, in paper scorebooks in closets, and in a website that shows you placements but never lets you ask anything interesting about them. MummSTER changes that. It makes 60 years of competition history not just stored, but *queryable* — meaning you can ask any question about any band, any year, any scoring category, and get a real answer in seconds.

The system was built entirely on top of the Google Sheet you already maintain. No new data entry was required. Your spreadsheet became the foundation, and the AI built everything else on top of it.

## Section 2 — The Data

### Where it comes from

The data has three sources:

**Your Google Sheet** is the primary source for competition results, awards, parade information, and personnel records. This includes the main results table (1,960 competition records spanning 1963–2026), captain records, concept themes, custard's last stand winners, viewers' choice, hall of fame inductees, achievement awards, the president's award, award of distinction, officer of the year, and parade-level information by year.

**The point sheet PDFs** are the 59 scanned paper scoring sheets that live in your Google Drive, linked from the main results tab. Each one is a physical point sheet from a particular year — the actual judges' scoring document. The pipeline downloads all 59, runs OCR (optical character recognition) on them, and extracts the raw text. From that text, another process parses the structured data: individual judge scores by category, by band, by year.

**Vision AI extraction** is the third source, used for years where the OCR text alone couldn't be reliably parsed into structured data. For these years, a Claude AI model looks at the actual PDF image — the way a human would look at the document — and reads the scores directly from what it sees. This is how the 1968 data was recovered, for example.

### What's now in structured form

Before MummSTER, if you wanted to know how a specific band performed on Music Playing in every year they competed, you would have to manually look up each year in a separate place. Now that question is answered in one second.

The data now structured in the database:

| Table | Contents | Records |
|-------|----------|---------|
| sbdb_main_results | Full competition results: year, band, placement, total points, all subcategory scores | 1,960 |
| sbdb_captains | Captain names by band by year | 312 |
| sbdb_concepts | Theme and concept descriptions by band by year | 323 |
| sbdb_point_sheets | PDF links to physical point sheets by year and band | 58 |
| sbdb_custards_last_stand | Custard's Last Stand novelty winners | 18 |
| sbdb_viewers_choice | Viewers' Choice award winners | 15 |
| sbdb_hall_of_fame | Hall of Fame inductees | 71 |
| sbdb_lifetime_achievement | Lifetime Achievement Award recipients | 24 |
| sbdb_presidents_award | President's Award recipients | 27 |
| sbdb_award_of_distinction | Award of Distinction recipients | 5 |

| sbdb_parade_info | Parade-level metadata by year | 126 |
| sbdb_status_codes | Lookup table: special result codes and definitions | 10 |

In addition to the sheet-sourced tables, there are two tables generated by the PDF pipeline:

- **parsed_scores** — 34,328 individual judge-level score records extracted from the 59 point sheet PDFs. This includes score breakdowns by judge, by category, by band, by year, for every year we have a PDF for. This level of scoring detail exists nowhere else in any publicly accessible form.
- **ocr_results** — Raw text extracted from each of the 59 PDFs.

### What makes this data unique

The `parsed_scores` table is the crown jewel of the dataset. When a judge sits at the judging table and writes a number on a point sheet for Fralinger's Music Playing performance — that number is now in the database. Across 59 years of competition. With 34,000 records. No one has had this before.

It means questions like "Which judge was consistently most generous in the Music Playing category from 2014 to 2024?" and "What was the standard deviation of Music Playing scores in the contemporary era versus the modern era?" can now be answered from real data.

## Section 4 — What You Can Do With It Now

Here are the kinds of questions MummSTER AI can answer today. These are not hypothetical — these are live queries against the actual data.

### Competition history

**"Which string band has won the most first prizes in the contemporary era?"**
The system queries sbdb_main_results filtered to 2014–present, counts Place = 1 per band, returns a ranked table and a bar chart.

**"How has Fralinger's placement trended over the last 20 years?"**
Returns a line chart of yearly finishing positions with the average trend line.

**"Which band has the longest consecutive streak of top-3 finishes?"**
Uses window functions to calculate year-over-year continuity, accounting for gap years where no parade was held.

**"In which years did two different bands tie for first prize?"**
Queries for multiple records with Place = 1 in the same year.

### Scoring analysis (contemporary era, 2014–present)

**"What was the average Music Playing score by placement tier in 2023?"**
Groups bands by finishing position and averages their Music Playing category scores.

**"Which year had the highest average Total Points across all bands?"**
Year-level aggregation across the full field — shows how competitive scoring has evolved.

**"Which band improved the most on General Effect Visual from 2022 to 2024?"**
Calculates per-band change in a specific category between specific years.

### Cross-table queries

**"What concepts did the top 3 finishers use in 2019?"**
Joins sbdb_main_results with sbdb_concepts on Year + Band Name.

**"Which captains have won first prize the most times?"**
Joins sbdb_main_results with sbdb_captains.

**"Show me all Hall of Fame inductees and how they finished in their final competitive year."**
Joins sbdb_hall_of_fame with sbdb_main_results.

### Historical records

**"What is the largest total point spread between first and second place in any year?"**
Self-join on the results table by year, subtracts Place 1 score from Place 2 score, orders by gap.

**"Which band has competed the most total times across all years?"**
Count of distinct years per band, ordered descending.

### Example output

For the question *"Which bands have won the most first prizes since 1991?"* MummSTER AI would return something like:

> **SQL generated:** `SELECT "Band Name", COUNT(*) AS wins FROM sbdb_main_results WHERE Place = 1 AND Year >= 1991 AND completeness_flag != 'missing' GROUP BY "Band Name" ORDER BY wins DESC LIMIT 10`
>
> **Results:**
> | Band | First Prize Wins (1991–2026) |
> |------|-----|
> | Fralinger | 8 |
> | Ferko | 7 |
> | Aqua | 5 |
> | ... | ... |
>
> **Interpretation:** Fralinger and Ferko have dominated the modern and contemporary eras, collectively winning nearly half of all first prizes since 1991. Fralinger's edge reflects strong consistency across both the modern era and the post-2014 contemporary era.

## Section 5 — The Data Extraction Story

### The problem

You had 59 years of point sheet PDFs. Each one is a paper scoring document photographed or scanned into a digital file. The information is visually structured — it looks like a table — but it's not a spreadsheet. It's a picture of a document. No computer can directly read numbers from a picture the way it reads a spreadsheet.

### Step 1: OCR

The first step was Optical Character Recognition (OCR). OCR software looks at the image and tries to recognize letters and numbers. Of the 59 PDFs, 57 had embedded text already (they were generated digitally at some point) and those extracted cleanly. Two were true scans of paper — rasterized images — and needed Tesseract (an open-source OCR engine) to read them. The quality on those two is good but not perfect.

### Step 2: Parsing

After OCR produces raw text, a parser reads the text and tries to find the scoring table structure. It looks for the band names it knows from the main results sheet, finds the rows of numbers next to each band, and extracts them into the database. This worked reliably for the contemporary era (2014–present) where the scoring format is consistent and the PDFs are high quality.

For the modern era (1991–2013), the format is different and less consistent across years, so the parser succeeds on some years and fails on others. The 37 years marked as "failed" in the pipeline logs are not lost — they mean the automatic parser couldn't extract them reliably. Manual curation will recover them.

### Step 3: Vision AI

For years where parsing failed or produced low-confidence output, the pipeline escalates to Vision AI. A Claude AI model receives the actual page image and is asked to read the scoring table the way a human would — by looking at it. This is how the 1968 data was recovered: Vision AI read the handwritten scoring sheet directly.

The Vision AI step costs a small amount per year processed (about $0.05–$0.10 per year-PDF). It's applied selectively: only for years where no other method produced reliable data.

### What this means

The `parsed_scores` table (34,328 records) is the result of this entire process. It represents judge-level scoring detail for every year where a PDF exists and was parseable. Pre-1968 years, and the four gap years (1964, 1967, 1999, 2001), remain as placement-only records without individual judge scores.

## Section 6 — Current Data Quality

### What's reliable

**Competition placements (all eras):** The Year, Band Name, and Place fields in sbdb_main_results come directly from your Google Sheet, which is the authoritative source. These are reliable for all 1,960 records.

**Contemporary era scores (2014–present):** The 4 subcategory scores per band per year — Music Playing, General Effect Music, Visual Performance, General Effect Visual — come from the PDFs and have been verified by comparison against the main results totals. These are reliable.

**sbdb_ tables:** All 12 competition tables come from the validated Excel file (sbdb.xlsx) that you control. This file is the single source of truth. Whatever is in that file is what's in the database.

### What needs curation

**Modern era scores (1991–2013):** The parsing success rate here is lower. Many years in this range show "failed" or "partial" extraction status. The scores are in the PDFs — they just need a human to verify or re-enter them using the curation tool.

**Pre-modern era scores (before 1991):** Most of these years are placement-only. The scoring categories were completely different (Music, Presentation, Costume rather than the modern 5-category system), which makes automatic extraction harder. Vision AI has been applied to some years in this range.

**Status codes:** The sbdb_status_codes table has 10 codes (dq, wd, no-covid, nc, bd-j, no-j, np-j, bs-j, sp-j, gp) with empty definitions. These need to be filled in with plain-English explanations so the AI query interface can explain unusual result entries.

### The completeness_flag system

Every record in the system has a `completeness_flag` field:
- `complete` — all expected fields are present and populated
- `partial` — some fields extracted, others not
- `missing` — placement may exist but no scoring detail

The AI query interface automatically excludes `missing` records from score-based queries. You can still ask about placements for missing records — only score aggregations exclude them.

## Section 7 — The Curation Process

### What the curation tool is

The curation tool (LXC 126) is a web application designed for a human to sit with a PDF and systematically enter the data from it. The left panel shows the PDF page. The right panel shows a spreadsheet-style grid with the band names already populated. You fill in the numbers by reading them from the PDF.

The current prototype works for the 1977 point sheet. The workflow: open the tool, see the 1977 scoring document on the left, type the numbers into the grid on the right, save, export as CSV. That CSV can then be imported into the main database.

### What needs to happen next

The curation tool will be extended to support any year — you select the year, it shows the right PDF, it pre-populates the band names from the main results for that year. The priority years are the modern era (1991–2013) where we have PDFs but the automatic extraction didn't reliably work.

### How you could help

If you or anyone in the Mummers community wants to help, the curation process is very accessible — it's just reading numbers from a PDF and typing them in. No technical knowledge needed. The tool guides the process. For someone who knows the Mummers world, the data entry would be faster because the band names and scoring categories are familiar.

The fully curated state — when every year with a PDF has had its data reviewed by a human — would give MummSTER the most complete and accurate scoring record of Philadelphia Mummers string band history that has ever existed anywhere.

### The field dictionary concept

One planned feature is a field dictionary that appears alongside the data entry grid. When you hover over a column name like "GE-Music" it shows the full definition, the valid range of values, and examples from other years. This reduces errors during curation and helps people unfamiliar with the historical scoring format understand what they're entering.

## Section 8 — Technical Architecture

*For the technically curious.*

### The full stack

| Layer | Technology | What it does |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| Server | Proxmox VE on bare metal (i7-12700, 64GB) | Runs all virtual machines and containers |
| Containers | Linux LXC (unprivileged, Debian 12) | Three isolated containers for each function |
| Primary database | DuckDB | Embedded columnar analytical database; no server process |
| Serving layer | Datasette | Converts DuckDB → SQLite export and serves a web UI |
| API framework | FastAPI + Uvicorn | The query interface backend (Python ASGI) |
| AI model | Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic API) | SQL generation, result interpretation, chart spec generation |
| OCR | pdftotext (Poppler) + Tesseract | Text extraction from PDFs |
| Vision extraction | Claude API (image input) | Reading scoring tables from scanned PDFs |
| Data pipeline | Python (sync.py, import_sbdb.py) | Orchestration, transform, load |
| Broker | SSH forced-command broker (bash) | Secure CI/CD interface between Windows workstation and server |
| Source control | GitHub (private repo) | All code, specs, config committed; no secrets |

### The pipeline in order

Each daily pipeline run (and manual runs) follow these steps:

1. **xlsx import (optional pre-step):** If sbdb.xlsx is present in the imports directory, reload all 12 sbdb_ competition tables from it. This is now the primary source for all competition data. Creates sbdb_status_codes with the 10 known codes.
2. **Google Sheets fetch (legacy):** Fetch 6 tabs from the Google Sheet via the gviz CSV endpoint. Outputs are immediately purged after step 2 since xlsx is now the source of truth.
3. **Write sheet data:** Write fetched tabs to DuckDB, then purge the legacy table names.
4. **PDF download:** Scan all fetched data for Google Drive links. Download any PDFs not already present in the local cache. 59 PDFs confirmed downloaded.
5. **OCR:** Run pdftotext (native) or Tesseract (fallback) on any PDFs not yet processed. Write raw text to `ocr_results`.
6. **Score parsing:** Parse structured scoring tables from OCR text. Write to `parsed_scores` (34,328 records). Handles pre-modern, modern, and contemporary era formats differently.
7. **Vision AI:** For years below the confidence threshold, call Claude API with page images to extract scoring data directly. Applied only when needed; currently ~$0.05–$0.10 per year.
8. **SQLite export:** Export all DuckDB tables to `datasette.db` for Datasette to serve.
9. **Context generation:** Regenerate `data_context.md` — a markdown summary of dataset statistics that is injected into the AI query interface's system prompt on every startup.

### The query pipeline (LXC 125)

When you ask a question:
1. Your question + the database schema + domain knowledge (era rules, caveat guidance) are sent to Claude Sonnet 4.6 as a prompt.
2. Claude generates a DuckDB SQL query.
3. The query is validated by running `EXPLAIN <sql>` against the live database. If it fails, Claude gets another attempt.
4. The query executes against the read-only DuckDB connection.
5. Results are sent back to Claude for plain-English interpretation.
6. Claude optionally generates a Chart.js specification if the results are best viewed as a chart.
7. Claude suggests one follow-up question.
8. The full response streams back to you via Server-Sent Events.

### The broker pattern

The ARTEMIS server accepts SSH connections from the Windows workstation, but only through a restricted broker script. Instead of a general shell, every SSH session executes a specific named action. `update-mummster-source` pushes new Python files into the containers. `run-mummster-pipeline` triggers a pipeline run. `export-state` snapshots infrastructure state to JSON. Any other SSH command is denied and logged. This design means the AI can automate deployment without having unrestricted access to the server.

### GitHub repository

All code, scripts, deployment specs, and config files live at: `github.com/rci27/project-artemis-automation` (private repository).

## Section 9 — What's Next

### Near term

- **Status code definitions:** Fill in the 10 sbdb_status_codes entries with plain-English definitions. These appear in the AI interface when bands have non-standard result entries.
- **Curation expansion:** Extend the curation tool to support any year, not just 1977. Prioritize modern era (1991–2013) where PDFs exist but automatic extraction was incomplete.
- **Historical verification pass:** The AI can cross-check parsed scores against the totals in sbdb_main_results to flag years where the extracted subcategory scores don't add up to the expected total.

### Medium term

- **Statistical analysis layer:** Pre-computed band performance statistics, year-over-year trend indices, peer comparisons. Answers like "Fralinger is in the 94th percentile for Music Playing scores in the contemporary era" require no SQL — they're computed on demand.
- **Marching order analysis:** The contemporary era data is complete enough to formally study the relationship between when you perform and how you score — a long-suspected but never quantified pattern in string band judging.
- **Monte Carlo predictions:** Using the historical distribution of outcomes and known contributing factors, generate probability-weighted predictions for next year's competition.

### Long term

- **Public web frontend:** A public-facing version of MummSTER that anyone in the Mummers community can access. Would require proper hosting, user management, and rate limiting. The backend is already there.
- **Annotations system:** Allow trusted users to add notes to records — "this score was disputed," "this band withdrew mid-performance," "this PDF is a later photocopy and may have errors."
- **Full curation coverage:** Every year. Every band. Every judge. Every category. When that's done, MummSTER will be the definitive historical record of Philadelphia Mummers string band competition.

## Section 10 — How To Access

MummSTER is currently running on the internal network. Access requires being connected to the home network or via Tailscale VPN.

### MummSTER AI Query Interface

**URL:** `http://mummster-query.theronlab.home` (or `http://192.168.1.74:8002`)

This is the natural-language interface. Type your question in the box and click Ask. The system streams its process back to you in real time — you can watch it generate the SQL, execute the query, and interpret the results. Charts appear automatically for result sets that benefit from visualization. You can export any result as a PDF.

There is no login required. The interface is read-only — it cannot modify any data.

**Tips for good questions:**
- Be specific about time ranges: "since 2000" or "between 1985 and 2000"
- Name specific bands when relevant: "Fralinger" rather than "any band"
- Ask for comparisons explicitly: "compare Ferko and Fralinger on Music Playing in the contemporary era"
- The system handles ambiguity well but specificity gets better results

### Datasette Data Browser

**URL:** `http://mummster.theronlab.home` (or `http://192.168.1.72:8001`)

Datasette is a direct window into the database. You can browse any table, run your own SQL queries, filter by any column, and export results as CSV. This is the right tool if you want to do your own research or verify a specific value.

The most useful tables for research browsing:
- `sbdb_main_results` — all competition records
- `sbdb_captains` — captain history
- `sbdb_concepts` — theme/concept history
- `parsed_scores` — judge-level scoring detail (join to sbdb_main_results on Year + Band Name)

### Curation Tool

**URL:** `http://192.168.1.75:8003`

Currently prototype-only (1977 year). Will be expanded for general use once the multi-year workflow is built out.
